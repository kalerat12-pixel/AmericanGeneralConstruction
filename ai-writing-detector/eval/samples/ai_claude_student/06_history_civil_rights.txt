The Montgomery Bus Boycott, which began in December 1955, was one of the most important events of the civil rights movement. It started when Rosa Parks, a Black seamstress and NAACP member, refused to give up her seat to a white passenger on a city bus. Her arrest sparked a citywide protest that lasted more than a year.

The boycott was carefully organized by local leaders, including a young minister named Martin Luther King Jr., who was chosen to lead the Montgomery Improvement Association. Black residents, who made up the majority of bus riders, refused to ride the buses. Instead, they walked, shared rides, and organized carpools, sometimes traveling many miles each day to get to work.

The boycott put serious economic pressure on the bus company and drew national attention to segregation in the South. Many participants faced harassment, job losses, and even violence. King's house was bombed, but he urged his supporters to respond with nonviolence rather than anger.

In November 1956, the Supreme Court ruled in Browder v. Gayle that bus segregation was unconstitutional. The boycott officially ended in December 1956 when the buses were integrated.

The Montgomery Bus Boycott showed that ordinary people, working together, could challenge unjust laws. It also launched Martin Luther King Jr. into national leadership and inspired future protests across the country. Its success proved that organized, nonviolent resistance could be a powerful tool for social change.
